Inside Atlas.

Notes on why we're building a document environment for macOS, and the decisions shaping it so far.

August 2026 Atlas 5 min read

Atlas started from a small complaint: most document apps make you think about the app before they let you think about the document.

Toolbars stacked three rows deep. Panels that open on their own. Settings that quietly change how a page behaves depending on which mode you happen to be in. We wanted something calmer — a place where writing, sketching, and organizing ideas could share the same window without competing for it.

That's the short version of why Atlas exists. The longer version is really a set of small decisions, and this note is about a few of them.

One document, many forms.

Early on we tried building Atlas as three separate modes — a text editor, a canvas, and an outliner — switched with a segmented control at the top of the window. It worked, but it felt like three small apps wearing one coat.

The current approach treats a document as a single continuous space. Text can sit next to a sketch. An outline can live beside a paragraph it's structuring. Nothing forces you to declare, upfront, what kind of document you're making.

  • Text blocks that can be reordered like an outline
  • A lightweight canvas layer for sketches and diagrams
  • Everything searchable from a single index, regardless of type

It's a harder model to build well. It's also the only one that matched how we actually work — jumping between writing and drawing without opening a new file to do it.

Native, on purpose.

Atlas is being built directly on AppKit rather than a cross-platform framework. That's a slower path, and it means fewer things come for free. But it also means the little things — scroll physics, trackpad gestures, window resizing, how text feels under your cursor — behave the way macOS users already expect, without us having to reinvent them.

"If it doesn't feel native, it doesn't matter how clever the feature underneath it is."

We've rebuilt the sidebar three times so far, mostly because the first two versions were technically fine but felt slightly foreign — a little too eager, a little too animated. The third version does less, and it's the one we kept.

What's still unresolved.

We don't have a good answer yet for how version history should work across mixed content — text and canvas elements evolve differently, and most history models assume one or the other. It's the part of Atlas we're spending the most time on right now.

Sync between devices is designed but not yet built. Collaboration isn't on the roadmap for the first release — we'd rather ship something small and correct than something broad and shaky.

Atlas is still in development, and it will likely look somewhat different by the time it ships. But the core idea — one calm space for a document to take whatever shape it needs — hasn't changed since the first sketch.
